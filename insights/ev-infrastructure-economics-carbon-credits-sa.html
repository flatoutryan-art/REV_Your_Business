EV Infrastructure Economics & Verra Carbon Offsets in South Africa

8 min read·REV Improvement Consulting

Electric vehicles in South Africa are often argued about as a lifestyle choice. For fleets, they are a cost and carbon question with real numbers behind it. This article sets out the practical economics, then walks through a transparent worked example of how replacing petrol motorcycles can generate Verra Verified Carbon Units (VCUs).

Infrastructure: Bottlenecks and Off-Grid Pioneers

The constraint on EV adoption here is rarely the vehicle. It is reliable charging: grid supply interruptions, uneven public charger coverage outside the main corridors, and depot connections that need upgrading. One response is the growth of off-grid charging networks supplied by photovoltaic (PV) power, such as Zero Carbon Charge, which can operate independently of grid availability and serve routes where grid connections are slow to arrive. For fleet operators, the realistic choice is usually a mix: depot charging on grid supply, supplemented by decentralized microgrid supply or PV-backed public charging where uptime matters.

Total Cost of Ownership: Fuel Versus Charging

Pump prices remain volatile, and the case for electrification is best tested against higher fuel-price scenarios rather than today's price alone. The table below uses illustrative assumptions, not quotes or forecasts, for a light delivery motorcycle:

ScenarioPetrol ICE motorcycle (3 L/100 km)Electric motorcycle (about 4.75 kWh/100 km)
Fuel at R30 per litre / charging at R4 per kWhabout R90 per 100 kmabout R19 per 100 km
Fuel at R40 per litre / charging at R8 per kWhabout R120 per 100 kmabout R38 per 100 km

The running-cost gap is large under both scenarios. A proper fleet model must then add the other side of the ledger: purchase price, battery replacement, charging infrastructure, downtime and residual values. Running-cost savings are the headline, but they only become a business case once those items are priced.

Worked Example: Carbon Credits From One Electric Motorcycle

Verra credits are measured in tonnes of CO2e, and one VCU equals one tonne. The example below shows the logic REV uses when screening a diversion project. All inputs are stated so they can be challenged:

  • Usage: a full 3.8 kWh battery charged per day, 300 operating days per year, gives 1,140 kWh of charging per year. At roughly 80 km per charge, that is about 24,000 km.
  • Charging mix: 80% Eskom grid and 20% PV. Using an illustrative grid factor of about 0.95 kg CO2e per kWh and treating PV as near zero, charging emissions are roughly 0.87 tCO2e per year.
  • Baseline: a petrol motorcycle consuming 3.0 to 3.2 L/100 km over the same 24,000 km burns about 720 to 770 litres, or roughly 1.7 to 1.8 tCO2e at about 2.3 kg CO2e per litre.
  • Net reduction: roughly 0.8 to 0.9 tCO2e per motorcycle per year, which is about one VCU or slightly less.

The important lesson is scale. One motorcycle is a rounding error. A fleet of around 1,000 comparable motorcycles would generate roughly 800 to 900 VCUs per year in this model, before buffer deductions, monitoring and verification costs. Whether those credits can actually be issued depends on an approved methodology, a defensible additionality case, a credible baseline for what each rider would otherwise have used, per-vehicle monitoring data, and proof that the same reduction is not claimed elsewhere. These figures are an illustrative screening model, not a verified issuance.

Fleet Decarbonization and Regulatory Pressure

As far as we are aware, South Africa has not legislated a phase-out date for combustion vehicles. Pressure on legacy fleets arrives indirectly: carbon pricing, lender and customer ESG requirements, and export-market rules for businesses selling into carbon-regulated economies. Fleets that begin measuring and diverting fuel use now build the data trail that credit registration, ESG reporting and carbon tax planning all require. Our guides to Verra registration and South Africa's carbon tax Phase 2 cover those downstream steps.
