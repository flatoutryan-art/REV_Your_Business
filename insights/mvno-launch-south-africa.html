What It Actually Takes to Launch an MVNO in South Africa

2026-07-30·7 min read·REV Improvement Consulting

South Africa's telecoms market has room for MVNOs (Mobile Virtual Network Operators) that solve a specific, underserved need — but the gap between "we have a licence" and "we have a functioning, profitable network business" is wider than most first-time entrants expect.

The Network Agreement Is the Foundation, Not a Formality

Every MVNO rides on a host network operator's infrastructure. The commercial terms of that agreement — wholesale rates, data prioritisation, SLA guarantees, and exclusivity clauses — determine your margin structure for the life of the business before you've signed a single customer. Getting this wrong is very difficult to renegotiate later from a position of strength, because by then the host network holds all the leverage.

This is where experienced negotiation matters most. The businesses that get favourable host agreements are usually the ones that came to the table with a credible commercial plan and a realistic view of volume projections, not an optimistic one.

Billing and Provisioning Systems Are Where Budgets Actually Disappear

The unglamorous back-end — billing accuracy, real-time provisioning, number porting, RICA compliance — is where MVNO launches quietly bleed money and customer trust. A billing system that can't handle promotional pricing correctly, or a provisioning delay that takes three days instead of three minutes, does more damage to customer retention than any competitor's marketing campaign.

Budgeting realistically for this layer, and testing it under real load before public launch, is consistently underestimated in first-time MVNO business plans.

Positioning Against Incumbents Requires an Honest Answer

South Africa's mobile market already has strong incumbent operators. A new MVNO needs an honest, specific answer to "why would someone switch to us" that goes beyond price — because pure price competition against operators with vastly larger scale is rarely sustainable. The MVNOs that carve out real market share tend to serve a segment the incumbents structurally can't or won't serve well: a specific industry vertical, a underserved geography, or a bundled product incumbents don't offer.

The Regulatory Path Is Manageable — If Sequenced Correctly

ICASA licensing, numbering allocation, and RICA compliance are all navigable, but the sequencing matters. Applying for licensing before the host network agreement is commercially locked in, for example, can leave a business holding a licence with no viable network deal to activate it against. Getting the sequence right — commercial agreements, technical integration, then regulatory finalisation — saves months of rework.

What Separates Launches That Scale From Launches That Stall

The MVNO builds that go on to scale share a few common traits: a tightly defined target segment from day one, a host agreement negotiated with real commercial leverage, and back-end systems tested under realistic load before the first customer signs up. The ones that stall usually got the marketing right and the operational foundation wrong — and by the time that becomes obvious, it's an expensive problem to fix.
